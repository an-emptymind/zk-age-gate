Part I · Chapter 1

The impossible request

You want to convince someone that a statement is true. They do not trust you. And you refuse to show them why it is true.

That sounds like a contradiction, so let's make it concrete with something from your own world.

You hold an address with 1,000 ETH. You want to enter a token sale that is open only to whales. The sale contract wants proof. The obvious move is to sign a message from that address — but now the operator knows exactly which address you are, can watch every trade you make forever, and can be subpoenaed for that list later.

What you actually want to say is: "There exists an address I control whose balance exceeds 1,000 ETH." Nothing more. Not which address. Not the exact balance. Not a signature that links the two.

A zero-knowledge proof is a protocol that lets you say exactly that sentence and nothing else — and it is not a trick of obfuscation. It is a mathematical guarantee that the verifier learns literally zero bits beyond the truth of the statement.

Every ZK proof in this course proves a statement of the same shape. Get comfortable with it now, because everything from Chapter 2 to Chapter 21 is a variation on it:

Part I · Chapter 2

Ali Baba's cave

The story every cryptographer tells first — because it contains the entire idea with no maths at all.

There is a ring-shaped cave with a single entrance. Deep inside, the ring is blocked by a magic door that opens only to a secret password. From the entrance you cannot see the door, and you cannot see which way anyone walked.

Peggy (the prover) claims she knows the password. Victor (the verifier) wants proof, but Peggy refuses to say the password out loud. So they do this:

  1. Victor waits outside. Peggy walks in and takes either the left or the right passage — Victor doesn't see which.
  2. Victor walks to the fork and shouts a random side: "Come out on the left!"
  3. Peggy comes out on the side he asked for.

If she took the wrong branch, the only way back is through the door — which needs the password. So an honest Peggy always succeeds. A liar has to guess Victor's shout in advance: she succeeds with probability ½.

Once is not convincing. Twenty times in a row is: a liar's chance of surviving is 1 in 1,048,576. Run it and watch.

Part I · Chapter 3

The three rules

A protocol earns the name "zero-knowledge proof" only if it satisfies three properties. Every one of them was on display in the cave.

Before the properties, look again at the shape of the conversation. Every interactive ZK protocol is the same three moves — and you have already run them by hand in the cave. Each arrow below carries both readings: the general name on top, what it was in the cave underneath.

The order is the whole trick, and the cave shows you why. Swap moves 1 and 2 — let Victor shout before Peggy walks in — and the protocol collapses instantly: she just strolls down the branch he named, password or no password. The proof works only because she is already committed, in the dark, when the challenge arrives.

Keep that pairing in mind for the rest of the course. In Chapter 9 Peggy's "passage" becomes a number R = gr, Victor's "shout" becomes a random c, and "walking out the right side" becomes s = r + c·x. Different costume, identical play.

1 · Completeness — honesty always works

If the statement is true and both parties follow the protocol, the verifier accepts. Every time, not most of the time. In the cave: knowing the password means you can always exit where asked.

2 · Soundness — lying almost never works

If the statement is false, no cheating prover can make the verifier accept, except with some tiny probability we call the soundness error. In the cave one round has error ½; forty rounds bring it to 2⁻⁴⁰. In real systems we target 2⁻¹⁰⁰ or better.

Note the asymmetry: completeness is absolute, soundness is statistical. A ZK proof is not "this is true" — it is "the odds I fooled you are smaller than the odds this server is hit by a meteor mid-verification."

3 · Zero-knowledge — the verifier learns nothing new

This is the subtle one, and it has a beautiful formal definition. We say the verifier learns nothing if there exists a simulator: a program that produces transcripts indistinguishable from real ones, without knowing the witness at all.

In the cave, the simulator is a film crew. Hire an actress who knows no password, point a camera at the entrance, and film her walking in and out. Whenever she happens to emerge from the side that was shouted, keep the take; otherwise cut it. Splice the good takes together and you have a tape of someone passing twenty rounds in a row — indistinguishable from a tape of the real Peggy, and containing no password, because there was none. That is why a real transcript teaches you nothing: a fake one looks exactly the same.

Think about why that's exactly the right definition. If a fake transcript and a real transcript are indistinguishable, then anything you could compute from the real one, you could have computed from the fake one — and the fake one never touched the secret. Therefore the real one gave you nothing. We will build a working simulator with our own hands in Chapter 10.

Vocabulary you'll meet in papers

Argument vs proof: a "proof" is sound even against a prover with unlimited computing power; an "argument" is sound only against a polynomial-time prover. Nearly every practical SNARK is an argument — it relies on the prover being unable to break discrete log. The "ARK" in SNARK literally stands for ARgument of Knowledge.

Proof of knowledge: stronger than proving a statement is true. It proves the prover possesses the witness — formalised by an extractor, Chapter 10.

Honest-verifier ZK: zero-knowledge only if the verifier picks challenges randomly as specified. Enough for us, because after Fiat–Shamir (Chapter 11) a hash function plays the verifier and is honest by construction.

Part I · Chapter 4

Proving a hard problem

The cave is a toy. Here is a real zero-knowledge proof for a genuinely hard problem — and it is still simple enough to play with by hand.

Graph 3-colouring: given a network of nodes and edges, colour every node with one of three colours so that no edge connects two nodes of the same colour. Finding such a colouring is NP-complete — brute force on a big graph is hopeless.

Peggy has found a valid colouring and wants to prove it exists without revealing it. The protocol:

  1. Peggy randomly permutes the three colours across the whole graph (swap all reds for greens, etc.) — still valid, but it tells you nothing about the original.
  2. She puts a sealed hat over every node. Committed: she can't change anything now.
  3. Victor picks one edge at random and lifts only those two hats.
  4. He checks the two colours differ. Then everything is re-hatted and re-permuted for the next round.

Victor sees two different colours. Since they were re-randomised, that is the only thing he learns — he could have guessed "two different colours out of three" himself. But if Peggy's colouring were invalid, at least one edge is bad, and he had a 1-in-11 chance of picking it. Enough rounds and she's caught.

Aside: what “NP-complete” means

That phrase is doing heavy lifting, so let's unpack it. It rests on a gap you already know from mining: finding an answer is hard, checking one is easy.

ProblemFinding the answerChecking a given answer
Sudoku (n×n)brutalscan each row and column — instant
3-colouringexponential blow-uplook at each edge — instant
Factoring an RSA modulusinfeasibleone multiplication
“I know this address's key”infeasibleone scalar multiplication

NP is the class of problems where, once someone hands you the answer, you can verify it quickly. It stands for nondeterministic polynomial — it does not mean “not polynomial”, and every easy problem is also in NP (P ⊆ NP).

NP-complete problems are the hardest members of NP, with one remarkable extra property: every other problem in NP can be mechanically rewritten as an instance of it, in polynomial time. That rewriting is a reduction. Graph 3-colouring, SAT, knapsack and circuit satisfiability are all NP-complete — so they are all the same problem wearing different clothes. Solve any one efficiently and you have solved all of them, and collected a $1M Millennium Prize, since P vs NP is still open.

Why this is a big deal

A ZK proof proves “I know a w such that R(x, w) holds, where R is fast to check” — which is precisely the definition of NP. So NP is exactly the set of statements that can have zero-knowledge proofs, and since 3-colouring is NP-complete, this little hat game proves a sweeping theorem: everything with an efficiently checkable answer has a zero-knowledge proof. Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson proved it in 1986, and it is the licence under which the rest of this course operates.

You will meet the same idea again in Part IV. R1CS is circuit satisfiability, another NP-complete language — which makes Circom a reduction engine, mechanically rewriting your statement into the one form the proof system knows how to handle. All that remains is making it fast and non-interactive.

Part II · Chapter 5

Clock arithmetic

All of cryptography happens inside finite worlds where numbers wrap around. If you can read a clock, you already understand this.

It is 10 o'clock. You add 5 hours. It is 3 o'clock — not 15. You just computed 15 mod 12 = 3. Modular arithmetic is that, with a dial of any size.

In ZK we use a dial whose size is a prime number p, and the set {0, 1, …, p−1} is called a finite field, written 𝔽p. Primes matter for one reason: on a prime dial, every non-zero number has a multiplicative inverse, so you can divide. On a 12-dial you cannot divide by 4; on a 13-dial you can divide by anything.

Part II · Chapter 6

One-way streets

Cryptography needs operations that are trivial forwards and hopeless backwards. Here is the one that powers Schnorr, ECDSA, and every SNARK you will deploy.

Pick a prime p and a base g. Given a secret exponent x, computing

y = gx mod p

takes microseconds, even for a 2048-bit exponent, using repeated squaring. Going the other way — given g, p, y, find x — is the discrete logarithm problem, and after fifty years of attacks the best known algorithms are still hopeless at cryptographic sizes.

The wrap-around is what destroys the information. Over the real numbers, 2ˣ grows smoothly and you can binary-search for x. Mod p, the outputs bounce around unpredictably — x and x+1 land nowhere near each other. Sorting or interpolating gives you nothing.

That gap — cheap one way, catastrophic the other — is the entire engine. A zero-knowledge proof is, at bottom, a way of doing algebra on the cheap side of a one-way street so that the verifier can check your work without ever crossing to the expensive side.

Part II · Chapter 7

Groups, generators, and why we switched to curves

One word does the heavy lifting in every ZK paper: group. Rather than define it, let's look at one small enough to print in full.

A group is a playground you can't fall out of

Take the numbers {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} and one operation: multiply, then wrap mod 7. Here is the entire world, every possible product:

multiplication mod 7 — cells highlighted where the answer is 1

Stare at that table for a moment, because it contains all four group rules:

  • Closed — every answer in the grid is still one of 1…6. You can multiply forever and never escape.
  • Identity — the row for 1 is unchanged: 1 × a = a. One element does nothing.
  • Inverses — a 1 appears exactly once in every row. 2×4=8=1, 3×5=15=1, 6×6=36=1. Every element has a partner that undoes it, so you can always divide.
  • Associative — brackets don't matter: (2×3)×4 = 2×(3×4). Multiplication was always like this.

That is the whole definition. A group is a set plus an operation with those four properties — nothing more mysterious than the times table above.

A generator is a number that visits everybody

Now pick one element and keep multiplying it by itself. Start with 3:

3¹ = 3      3² = 9  = 2      3³ = 3×2 = 6
3⁴ = 3×6 = 4      3⁵ = 3×4 = 5      3⁶ = 3×5 = 1   ← back to the start

Six steps, and it touched every single element {3,2,6,4,5,1} before returning home. 3 is a generator: its powers sweep out the whole group. Now try 2:

2¹ = 2      2² = 4      2³ = 8 = 1   ← home already, after only 3 steps

2 only ever reaches {2, 4, 1}. It is trapped in a small loop and never visits 3, 5 or 6 — so it is not a generator. The number of steps before you get back to 1 is that element's order. Order of 3 is 6; order of 2 is 3.

This is the single most common source of "wait, is that a typo?" when reading ZK code. There are two different moduli in play:

  • The elements wrap at p — in our toy world, mod 7.
  • The exponents wrap at q, the order — mod 6, because 3⁶ = 1 sends you back to the start.

Exponents live on a smaller dial than elements. In Chapters 9–11 we use p = 1019, q = 509, g = 4 — so when Peggy computes s = (r + c·x) mod 509 but the group elements are mod 1019, that is this rule, not a mistake.

From numbers to curves

Here is the good news: you have already learned elliptic curves. They are the same playground with the operation renamed.

Think of both as a racetrack. You start at a fixed point and take x identical steps around it. The track wraps, so from where you finish, nobody can work out how many steps you took — that's the discrete log problem from Chapter 6. The only difference is what "one step" means:

  • With numbers: one step = multiply by g. After x steps you are at gx.
  • With a curve: one step = add the point G. After x steps you are at x·G.

"Adding" two points is a geometric rule — draw a line through them, find where it hits the curve again, reflect it — but you never need to do it by hand. Here is the real curve y² = x³ + 2x + 2 over 𝔽₁₇, walked the same way we walked the powers of 3:

G  = (5, 1)         // the starting point, like g
2G = (6, 3)         // like g²
3G = (10, 6)        // like g³
4G = (3, 1)
5G = (9, 16)        // …and so on, 19 points before it wraps

Given (9, 16), finding that it was 5 steps is the hard problem. With 17 points you can just count; with a 256-bit curve you cannot.

So why bother switching? Purely size. To reach 128-bit security with plain numbers mod p, you need p around 3072 bits, because a clever attack called index calculus chips away at them. That attack doesn't work on curves, so 256 bits is enough — the same security in an eighth of the space. On a chain where you pay per byte, that decides it.

The curves in your stack

secp256k1 — Ethereum signatures. Not pairing-friendly, so it can't be used for Groth16.

BN254 (alt_bn128) — the curve behind precompiles 0x06/0x07/0x08. Pairing-friendly, ~100-bit security after the 2016 attacks. This is what almost every deployed Ethereum verifier uses, because it's the only one with cheap precompiles.

BLS12-381 — stronger (~120-bit), used by Ethereum consensus and Zcash Sapling, but it has no EVM precompile yet, so verifying on L1 is expensive.

A pairing is a function e(P, Q) that lets you check products of secrets "inside the exponent" without learning them. You need it in Chapter 15; you don't need to know how it's built.

Part II · Chapter 8

Commitments: the sealed envelope

Peggy's hats in Chapter 4 were hand-waving. Here's how you actually build one.

A commitment scheme lets you lock in a value now and reveal it later, with two guarantees:

  • Hiding — the commitment reveals nothing about the value.
  • Binding — you cannot later open it to a different value.

A sealed envelope, in other words. And it's the reason step 1 of every protocol comes before the challenge in step 2.

The hash commitment

The simplest version: C = H(value ‖ r) where r is a large random blinding factor. The blinding matters enormously — without it, committing to "yes" or "no" is trivially broken by hashing both options and comparing. This is exactly the bug in naive commit-reveal voting on-chain.

Opening is a second message

A commitment always runs in two phases, and the second one is easy to miss:

phase 1 · commit   Peggy → Victor:  C = H(v ‖ r)    // only C crosses
phase 2 · open     Peggy → Victor:  v, r            // now the value crosses
                   Victor computes H(v ‖ r) himself and compares with the C he stored

So Victor never verifies anything from C alone — he can't. He verifies by redoing the hash once Peggy hands over the value and the blinding factor, which he can do because H is public and deterministic. What he cannot do is run it backwards.

That means hiding is temporary and binding is permanent: Victor learns nothing until Peggy opens, and when she opens, she can only open to the one value she originally sealed. A commitment is delayed revelation, not secrecy.

“But doesn't opening it defeat the whole point?”

If that just went through your head, good — it is the right objection, and the answer reframes everything that follows.

A commitment scheme is not a zero-knowledge proof. It is a primitive: one component that ZK protocols are built out of. Used on its own, as in the lab above, the value really does get revealed at the end — and that is fine, because in that setting the goal was never privacy. A sealed-bid auction wants the winning bid opened eventually; it just doesn't want anyone changing their bid after seeing the others.

Which points at the reframe: in a ZK protocol, a commitment's real job is binding, not hiding. It pins the prover down so she cannot change her answer after the challenge arrives. Hiding merely stops the envelope leaking while it waits. There are exactly three things a protocol ever does with one:

That last row is the one that matters, and you are two chapters from it. In Chapter 9 Peggy sends R = gr and never reveals r. Rather than unseal the envelope, she answers a question about its contents: s = r + c·x. Victor checks that the answer is consistent with the sealed R — gs = R·yc — without ever seeing inside it.

The one sentence to keep

The leap from “sealed envelope” to “zero-knowledge proof” is this: you answer a question about the secret instead of opening it. The commitment is what stops you from changing your story halfway through — nothing more, and nothing less.

Pedersen commitments — the ones ZK actually uses

Hash commitments are opaque: you can't do arithmetic on them. Pedersen commitments can be added together while still sealed, which is the property that makes confidential transactions and polynomial commitments possible.

C(m, r) = gm · hr mod p     // m = message, r = random blinder

h is a second generator whose discrete log relative to g nobody knows — that's essential, and it's your first encounter with a trusted setup. Then the magic:

C(m₁,r₁) · C(m₂,r₂) = gm₁+m₂·hr₁+r₂ = C(m₁+m₂, r₁+r₂)

Multiply two sealed envelopes and you get a sealed envelope containing the sum — without ever opening either. That's additive homomorphism. Verify it yourself:

Part III · Chapter 9

Schnorr's protocol

Your first real zero-knowledge proof with actual numbers. It proves "I know the private key for this public key" — and it is three lines of algebra.

Setup: group of order q with generator g mod p. Peggy's secret is x; her public key is y = gx mod p. Everyone knows g, p, q, y. Nobody but Peggy knows x.

  1. Commit. Peggy picks a random nonce r and sends R = gr mod p.
  2. Challenge. Victor sends a random c from {0 … q−1}.
  3. Respond. Peggy sends s = (r + c·x) mod q.
  4. Verify. Victor checks gs ≟ R · yc mod p.

Why it balances: gs = gr+cx = gr·(gx)c = R·yc. The exponents add up only if the x inside s is the same x inside y.

And why it hides: s = r + c·x where r is fresh uniform randomness. That's a one-time pad on the secret. Any value of s is equally likely regardless of x, so s alone leaks nothing.

Part III · Chapter 10

The extractor and the simulator

Two thought experiments that turn "it seems secure" into proof. They are the deepest ideas in this course, and both are small enough to build.

Soundness: the extractor (rewinding)

How do we prove Peggy really knows x? Imagine we could rewind her like a save-file. Run her once, get (R, c₁, s₁). Rewind to just after she sent R, and ask a different challenge c₂, getting s₂. If she answers both correctly with the same R:

s₁ = r + c₁·x        s₂ = r + c₂·x
s₁ − s₂ = (c₁ − c₂)·x
x = (s₁ − s₂) · (c₁ − c₂)⁻¹  mod q     // the secret falls out

So anyone who can answer two different challenges on the same commitment has the secret — we just computed it from their answers. This is called special soundness, and it's why the protocol is a proof of knowledge, not just of truth.

This is a real exploit, not just theory

Reuse the nonce r across two signatures and an attacker runs that extractor for free. It is exactly how the Sony PS3 signing key leaked in 2010, and how countless ECDSA wallets have been drained. In your code: never reuse a nonce, never use a weak RNG for one. The same algebra that proves your protocol sound will steal your key if you slip.

Zero-knowledge: the simulator

Now the other direction. A transcript is (R, c, s) and it's valid when gs = R·yc. Look at that equation again — nothing says you have to produce the parts in order. So cheat: pick s and c at random first, then solve for the R that makes it balance:

R = gs · y−c mod p

The resulting transcript passes verification perfectly, and it was produced by someone who has never seen x. Run both and compare — you cannot tell which is which:

All six verify. Three were made with the secret, three by the simulator, which never saw it. The distributions are identical — not "close", identical. That is perfect zero-knowledge.

Here is the punchline. If a transcript can be manufactured by someone with no secret, then holding a real transcript cannot possibly teach you anything about the secret — because you could have produced an indistinguishable one yourself. "Learns nothing" becomes a provable statement about computation, not a feeling.

And notice the one thing the simulator needs that a real prover doesn't: it must know c before choosing R. That's the fine print. It's why the ordering of the three moves is load-bearing, and why the next chapter — which hands challenge-generation to a hash function — has to be done very carefully.

Part III · Chapter 11

Fiat–Shamir: firing the verifier

Interactive protocols are useless on a blockchain — a contract can't play twenty rounds of ping-pong with your browser. One trick removes the verifier entirely.

What did Victor contribute? Exactly one thing: an unpredictable number chosen after the commitment. So replace him with a hash function:

c = H(g ‖ p ‖ y ‖ R ‖ message)  mod q

Since H is unpredictable, Peggy can't choose R to steer c — the only way to find a favourable pair is to grind hashes, which costs 2¹²⁸ work. The proof collapses into a single self-contained blob (R, s) that anyone, anywhere, can verify with no interaction. Peggy can post it on-chain and go offline.

Part IV · Chapter 12

From code to circuit

Schnorr proves one hard-coded fact. We want to prove "I ran this program correctly." Getting there needs one big translation step.

First, the word itself

Part IV builds a SNARK, and the acronym is a checklist rather than a name — every letter is something you have already met:

So a SNARK is Schnorr's idea, made non-interactive, generalised from one fixed statement to an arbitrary program, and made succinct. That third clause is the whole reason this chapter exists.

Dwell on succinctness, because without it the exercise is pointless — if checking a proof cost as much as re-running the computation, the verifier would simply re-run it. A Groth16 proof is ~200 bytes and ~230k gas whether the circuit had a thousand constraints or a hundred million. Perfectly flat. The cost has to land somewhere, and it lands on the prover:

proving     seconds to minutes · gigabytes of RAM · often a GPU
verifying   milliseconds · 200 bytes · constant, forever
The letter that isn't there

Notice what the acronym does not contain: zero-knowledge. It is a separate, optional property. A SNARK can be succinct and non-interactive while hiding nothing whatsoever — which is exactly what a zkRollup does, since all its transaction data is published. Add witness-hiding and you have a zk-SNARK. The industry says “zk” for both, which is why Chapter 19 calls the name a misnomer everyone has given up correcting. When you read a spec, check which one it actually means.

Programs become equations

A proof system has exactly one skill: checking that numbers satisfy equations. It cannot run your code, follow a branch, or read memory. So to prove “I ran this program correctly”, you first have to restate that sentence as “these numbers satisfy these equations”.

Think of how a teacher marks long division. They don't redo the sum — they check that each line follows from the one above it. Show your working, and checking becomes mechanical.

An arithmetic circuit is your program rewritten as exactly that: a list of working, one operation per line. Take the function we'll use for the rest of Part IV,

f(x) = x³ + x + 5

and run it with x = 3. The working looks like this:

line 1     9  =   3 × 3
line 2    27  =   9 × 3
line 3    30  =  27 + 3
line 4    35  =  30 + 5

Four lines. Each is a single multiplication or addition, and anyone can check any one of them without knowing how to cube a number. Verifying the whole computation has turned into verifying four trivial equations.

That is the entire idea of this chapter. “I ran the program correctly” becomes “here are some numbers, and they satisfy these four equations.” And since an equation can be checked as a relationship without exposing the values inside it, privacy comes along for free — which is what the machinery in Chapters 13 to 15 is for.

Flattening: writing the working out in advance

Producing those four lines is called flattening, and it is the first thing circom or noir does to your source code. It is the same working as above, but written with names instead of numbers — because the circuit has to be correct for every possible x, not just for 3:

sym1 = x    * x     // line 1 — one multiplication
y    = sym1 * x     // line 2 — one multiplication
sym2 = y    + x     // line 3 — an addition
out  = sym2 + 5     // line 4 — an addition

The rule when flattening is: keep breaking expressions down until no line has more than one multiplication. You can't write out = x*x*x + x + 5 as a single line, because the constraint format in the next chapter has room for exactly one multiplication per line. Hence the intermediate names sym1, y, sym2 — they exist purely to hold partial results, exactly like the numbers 9, 27 and 30 in the worked version.

Now run those lines with x = 3 and collect every value that appeared — the input, the output, and each intermediate. That list is the witness:

w = [ 1, out,  x, sym1,  y, sym2 ]
  = [ 1,  35,  3,    9, 27,   30 ]

The witness is the thing you keep secret. The four lines are public — they are just the shape of the program, and everyone knows you're computing x³ + x + 5. What you're hiding is the column of numbers underneath.

That leading 1 looks arbitrary and it is the one piece you should not try to justify yet: it's a slot that permanently holds the number one. Chapter 13 shows exactly what it's for — it's how a constraint mentions a constant like the + 5.

The cost model that will govern your life

In a circuit, multiplications cost, additions are nearly free. Circuit size is counted in constraints, and constraints ≈ multiplications. But there's a harsher rule: everything a normal program does cheaply is expensive here, because a circuit has no branches and no loops.

  • if (a) { X } else { Y } → compute both branches, then select: out = a·X + (1−a)·Y. You always pay for both.
  • Loops must be unrolled to a compile-time bound. No while (cond).
  • Comparisons like a < b need bit decomposition — roughly 254 constraints for one comparison.
  • keccak256 costs ~150,000 constraints. Poseidon, a hash designed for circuits, costs ~300. This is why ZK apps use funny hash functions.
Part IV · Chapter 13

R1CS: the universal constraint format

We have four lines of working. The proof machinery is fussy and wants every line in an identical shape, so it only ever has to know one trick. Here is that shape.

One shape for every line

The shape is:

( left ) × ( right ) = ( output )

Each of the three slots may hold any sum of witness values. But there is only ever one multiplication, the one in the middle, joining left to right. That restriction is what the name means: rank-1 constraint system.

Line 1 fits straight away — x × x = sym1. So does line 2. But look at line 3, y + x = sym2: it has no multiplication at all. The fix is to multiply by one:

( y + x ) × ( 1 ) = ( sym2 )

An addition squeezed into the multiplication shape. This is also the real reason additions are “free” in a circuit — they never need a constraint of their own, they just ride along inside somebody else's slot.

Saying which values go in which slot

Now, how do you tell the machinery “put x in the left slot”? You hand it a picker: one coefficient per witness position, saying how much of each value to take.

w = [  1, out,  x, sym1,  y, sym2 ]     // the witness, by name
    [  1,  35,  3,    9, 27,   30 ]     // …and by value

a = [  0,   0,  1,    0,  0,    0 ]     // "take x, nothing else"

To use a picker, multiply position by position and add everything up:

a · w  =  0·1 + 0·35 + 1·3 + 0·9 + 0·27 + 0·30  =  3

That's all a dot product is here — a way of selecting values out of the witness. So constraint 1, x × x = sym1, is just three pickers:

a = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]   picks x      →  3
b = [0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]   picks x      →  3
c = [0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0]   picks sym1   →  9

check:   3 × 3 = 9   ✓

And now the formal rule says something you can actually read:

(a · w) × (b · w) = (c · w)

“Whatever the left picker grabs, times whatever the right picker grabs, equals whatever the output picker grabs.”

What the leading 1 was for

Line 4 is sym2 + 5 = out. Where does that 5 come from? It isn't a witness value — it's a constant baked into the program. This is the job of the permanent 1 in slot 0:

a = [5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1]   →  5·1 + 1·sym2  =  5 + 30  =  35
b = [1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]   →  1
c = [0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0]   →  out          =  35

check:   35 × 1 = 35   ✓

Because slot 0 always holds the number one, putting a 5 in that position of the picker contributes a literal 5 to the sum. That is the whole trick, and it is why every R1CS witness you will ever see starts with a 1.

Part IV · Chapter 14

Polynomial magic

Checking a million constraints one by one is not succinct. Here is the single idea that makes ZK proofs small — and it fits on a sticky note.

Two polynomials of degree at most d that are different can agree on at most d points. So if you evaluate both at a randomly chosen point and they match, they are almost certainly the same polynomial everywhere. This is the Schwartz–Zippel lemma:

Pr[ P(z) = Q(z)  |  P ≠ Q,  z random in 𝔽 ]  ≤  d / |𝔽|

Think of it as a fingerprint. Two million-term polynomials, compared by one number. Play with it in a tiny field where collisions actually happen:

Now scale it to production numbers. A real circuit has d ≈ 2²⁶ and the field has |𝔽| ≈ 2²⁵⁴, so the failure probability is about 2⁻²²⁸. You are far likelier to be hit by a meteorite during the verification call.

Why this makes proofs succinct

Here's the leap. Encode all m constraints into polynomials, so that "every constraint holds" becomes "one polynomial identity holds". Then the verifier doesn't check m things — it checks the identity at one random point. Verification cost stops depending on circuit size. That's the S in SNARK.

Two supporting tools make it airtight:

  • Vanishing polynomial. Let Z(z) = (z−1)(z−2)…(z−m), zero exactly at the constraint indices. "P vanishes at all constraint points" ⟺ "Z divides P" ⟺ "there exists H with P = H·Z". A divisibility claim becomes a multiplication the verifier can spot-check.
  • Polynomial commitments. The prover must not learn z before committing to its polynomial, or it could cook one up that happens to match. So it first commits (a KZG commitment is one group element, ~48 bytes, for a polynomial of any degree), then receives z, then opens at z with a short proof. Fiat–Shamir supplies z as a hash of the commitments.
Part IV · Chapter 15

QAP, the setup, and the toxic waste

Assembling the pieces into Groth16 — and confronting the one genuinely uncomfortable assumption in the whole stack.

R1CS → QAP

We have matrices; we need polynomials. The bridge is interpolation. Assign each constraint an index (1, 2, 3, 4…). For each witness slot i, look down column i of matrix A and find the polynomial Ai(z) passing through those values at those indices. Same for B and C.

Now define, for a witness w:

A(z) = Σ wᵢ·Aᵢ(z)     B(z) = Σ wᵢ·Bᵢ(z)     C(z) = Σ wᵢ·Cᵢ(z)
P(z) = A(z)·B(z) − C(z)

At z = k, this evaluates to exactly "constraint k's left times right minus output". So the witness is valid ⟺ P is zero at every constraint index ⟺ Z(z) divides P(z). The prover computes the quotient H = P / Z and proves A·B − C = H·Z at a random secret point. Millions of constraints, one equation. That is a Quadratic Arithmetic Program.

Two different things called “curve”

The curves here are ordinary graphs of polynomials — plot z across, value up, get a line or a parabola. They have nothing to do with the elliptic curves of Chapter 7, which are a group for hiding numbers in exponents. Same word, unrelated jobs: the polynomial curves hold your computation, the elliptic curve hides it. They only meet in Stage 8, where the polynomial is evaluated at a point buried in an elliptic-curve exponent.

Seeing it

All of this is easier to believe once you watch it. Below are the real curves from the two-constraint circuit worked out in The Wizard's Gate — you don't need that example yet, just watch the shapes:

Everywhere else they mean nothing; those two points are the entire content. Second, P and H·Z lie exactly on top of one another when the witness is honest, and visibly separate when it isn't.

Groth16 needs the evaluation point τ to be secret from the prover, yet the prover must be able to evaluate at it. The resolution: someone picks τ, publishes gτ, gτ², gτ³, … (the structured reference string), and then destroys τ. You can evaluate any polynomial "in the exponent" from those powers, but you can't recover τ — discrete log again.

If anyone keeps τ — the toxic waste — they can forge proofs of false statements, undetectably, forever. Note carefully: they cannot break privacy, only soundness. In a shielded pool that means silently minting coins.

The fix is a multi-party ceremony. Each participant adds their own randomness to the previous output; τ is the product of everyone's contributions, so it stays secret if at least one participant was honest and destroyed their share. Zcash's original ceremony had 6 participants; Ethereum's KZG ceremony for EIP-4844 had over 141,000.

Part IV · Worked example

The Wizard's Gate

One tiny statement, carried through every single transformation in Part IV — from a sentence in English to three numbers on a blockchain. Small enough that you can check every step with a pencil.

Peggy arrives at a gate. Carved into the stone:

“Speak not the number. Prove only that you know it.
The number, times itself, plus itself, makes twelve.”

Peggy knows the number is 3, because 3×3 + 3 = 12. The gate knows only 12. If she shouts "three!", the gate opens — and so does it for the goblin listening behind the hedge. She needs to prove she knows it while saying nothing.

Here is that proof being built, stage by stage.

Stage 1

Show your working Chapter 12

Why: the gate can't run programs. It can only check arithmetic. So Peggy rewrites her calculation as a list of working, with at most one multiplication per line.

a   = x * x      // 3 × 3 = 9
out = a + x      // 9 + 3 = 12

Two lines, because x*x + x has one multiplication and one addition and they can't share a line. The name a exists only to hold the middle result.

Collect every number that appeared. That list is the witness:

slot   [  1,  out,   x,   a ]
w   =  [  1,   12,   3,   9 ]

The gate will see out = 12. Everything else stays in Peggy's pocket.

Stage 2

Force both lines into one shape Chapter 13

Why: line 1 multiplies, line 2 adds. The machinery ahead only knows one trick, so every line must look identical: (left) × (right) = (output).

Line 1 already fits. Line 2 has no multiplication — so it rides on a × 1:

c1:   (  x  ) × (  x  ) = (  a  )
c2:   ( x + a ) × (  1  ) = ( out )

Now say which witness values fill each slot, using pickers — one coefficient per slot:

slot        [  1,  out,   x,   a ]
w        =  [  1,   12,   3,   9 ]

c1   L = [0,0,1,0] → 3       R = [0,0,1,0] → 3      O = [0,0,0,1] → 9
     check:  3 × 3 = 9  ✓

c2   L = [0,0,1,1] → 3+9=12  R = [1,0,0,0] → 1      O = [0,1,0,0] → 12
     check:  12 × 1 = 12  ✓

L, R and O stand for left, right, output — the three slots. (Papers and Chapter 13 call them a, b, c; here that would collide with our intermediate value a, so we spell them out.)

Notice b = [1,0,0,0] in the second constraint — that is the leading 1 earning its keep, letting the picker produce a literal one.

Stage 3

Why not stop right here? Chapter 14, the motive

With two constraints, the gate could simply check both and be done. But a real circuit — proving a Merkle path, or a rollup batch — has millions. If verifying means walking the list, verification cost grows with the program, and the whole point of a SNARK evaporates.

So the goal for the next two stages is precise: collapse every constraint into a single equation, checkable in one shot.

Stage 4

Turn the table into curves Chapter 15

This is the stage everyone finds hardest. We will go one link at a time, and nothing will be used before it has been built.

Where we are

After Stage 2 we have a list of two constraints. Each one is three dot products against the witness:

c1:   (L·w) × (R·w) = (O·w)   →    3 × 3 = 9
c2:   (L·w) × (R·w) = (O·w)   →   12 × 1 = 12

A list has exactly one problem: the only way to check a list is to walk it. Two entries is fine; two million is not. We want to replace the list with a few fixed objects, so that checking becomes one action instead of m actions. Polynomials will be those objects.

Link 1 · Give each constraint an address

A function needs something to be a function of. So label the constraints with numbers: c1 sits at z = 1, c2 sits at z = 2.

The choice is arbitrary — any two distinct numbers would do. All we need is that each constraint has its own address, so that “constraint number k” becomes something we can hand to a function.

Link 2 · Write down what we want, before building it

We want three functions with one job each:

A(z)   hand it an address, it returns that constraint's LEFT value
B(z)   ...its RIGHT value
C(z)   ...its OUTPUT value

Because if we had those, this single line would say everything the list said:

A(z) × B(z) = C(z)      at z = 1 and at z = 2

Reading the dot products above, that pins down exactly what the three functions must do:

A(1) = 3     A(2) = 12          // the left values
B(1) = 3     B(2) = 1           // the right values
C(1) = 9     C(2) = 12          // the outputs

That is the whole target. Everything from here is construction work to hit it.

Link 3 · Why we can't just build A(z) directly

We could find the line through (1, 3) and (2, 12) right now and be finished. But there is a catch, and it is the reason for the next two links.

Those numbers 3 and 12 came out of the witness — and the witness changes with every proof. The circuit, meanwhile, is compiled once: its A, B and C are published in the proving key and must work for every witness anyone will ever use. We cannot bake one witness's numbers into them.

So the job splits in two:

  • At compile time — build one small polynomial per witness slot. These depend only on the circuit's wiring, never on any witness. Fixed forever.
  • At proving time — weight those by the actual witness values and add them up. That produces A(z).

Which answers the question that trips everyone at this point: why do we interpolate down the columns instead of across the rows? Because the columns are the witness slots. One polynomial per slot is precisely what lets the witness be applied afterwards instead of being frozen in.

Link 4 · Build one polynomial per slot (compile time)

Here is matrix A again — rows are constraints, columns are witness slots. Take each column and find the line through its two values:

             slot 1    out      x       a
c1 (z=1)        0       0       1       0
c2 (z=2)        0       0       1       1
                ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓
A_i(z)  =       0       0       1      z−1

Column a reads 0 then 1, so its two points are (1,0) and (2,1) — the line z−1. Column x reads 1 then 1, giving the flat line 1. The two empty columns give 0. (The arithmetic is worked out in full at the end of this stage — for now, just note that each column became a line.)

Same treatment for B and C:

B_i(z)  =      z−1      0      2−z      0
C_i(z)  =       0      z−1      0      2−z

These twelve little polynomials are the circuit. They never change, they contain no secrets, and they are what actually sits inside a .zkey file.

Link 5 · Weight them by the witness (proving time)

Now Peggy arrives with her witness. Multiply each slot's polynomial by that slot's value and add them together:

w    =  [  1,   12,    3,    9  ]
           1    out    x     a

A(z) = 1·(0)   + 12·(0)   + 3·(1)   + 9·(z−1)  =  9z − 6
B(z) = 1·(z−1) + 12·(0)   + 3·(2−z) + 9·(0)    =  −2z + 5
C(z) = 1·(0)   + 12·(z−1) + 3·(0)   + 9·(2−z)  =  3z + 6

Link 6 · Why that lands exactly on the target

This is the join. Read it slowly, because it is the thing that makes the whole construction legal.

By the way we built them, each slot's polynomial hands back that slot's matrix entry when you feed it a constraint's address — that is literally what interpolating through those points means:

A_i(1) = the entry in row c1, column i
A_i(2) = the entry in row c2, column i

So watch what happens at z = 1:

A(1)  =  Σ  wᵢ · A_i(1)                    // from Link 5
      =  Σ  wᵢ · (row c1's entry for slot i)  // from the line above
      =  L · w                             // which is the dot product from Stage 2

Weighting and interpolating commute. Dot-product first and then interpolate, or interpolate first and then weight — you land in the same place, because both operations are nothing but sums of products. That is the entire justification for splitting the work across compile time and proving time.

And so the target from Link 2 is hit, exactly:

A(1) = 9−6  = 3      B(1) = −2+5 = 3      C(1) = 3+6  = 9
       →  3 × 3 = 9        ✓ that is constraint 1

A(2) = 18−6 = 12     B(2) = −4+5 = 1      C(2) = 6+6  = 12
       →  12 × 1 = 12      ✓ that is constraint 2

Nothing was gained and nothing was lost. The constraints were two rows in a table; they are now two points on three curves. But Stage 5 can do something with curves that it could never have done with a table.

Appendix · the interpolation arithmetic, step by step

Link 4 skipped the working. Here is the full recipe — it is just m = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) and y = mx + c from school, with x renamed z.

// line through (z₁, v₁) and (z₂, v₂)
Step 1   slope         m = (v₂ − v₁) / (z₂ − z₁)
Step 2   point-slope   f(z) = v₁ + m·(z − z₁)
Step 3   expand        f(z) = m·z + (v₁ − m·z₁)
Step 4   check         put z₁ and z₂ back in and confirm you get v₁ and v₂

Our two addresses are always z₁ = 1 and z₂ = 2, so the slope's denominator is always 2 − 1 = 1. That makes the slope simply “second value minus first value”, and c = v₁ − m.

Appendix · the shortcut, and how it scales

There is a slicker route that explains why only four expressions ever show up. Build two special lines first:

L₁(z) = 2 − z      // worth 1 at z=1, and 0 at z=2
L₂(z) = z − 1      // worth 0 at z=1, and 1 at z=2

Any column is then just (its value at c1) × L₁ + (its value at c2) × L₂ — each switch contributes at its own address and nothing anywhere else. Since our entries are only 0 or 1, the whole table has just four possible outcomes:

Column readsBuilt asSimplifies to
0, 00·L₁ + 0·L₂0
1, 1L₁ + L₂ = (2−z) + (z−1)1

With more than two constraints

y = mx + c only works for two points. With m constraints you get m switches, each of degree m−1, each worth 1 at its own address and 0 at every other:

L_k(z) = Π  (z − j) / (k − j)        for every j ≠ k

Those are the Lagrange basis polynomials, and this is Lagrange interpolation. What makes it safe is uniqueness: through m points there is exactly one polynomial of degree below m, so nothing is lost and nothing is ambiguous. Everything here also happens mod p, so dividing by (k − j) means multiplying by a modular inverse from Chapter 5. In production an FFT replaces this formula and runs in m log m — which is why real circuit sizes are padded to powers of two.

Stage 5

Two constraints become one equation Chapter 15

Define the error curve — how far each constraint is from balancing:

P(z) = A(z)·B(z) − C(z)
     = (9z − 6)(−2z + 5) − (3z + 6)
     = (−18z² + 57z − 30) − (3z + 6)
     = −18z² + 54z − 36

"Both constraints hold" now means "P is zero at both homes":

P(1) = −18 + 54 − 36 = 0  ✓
P(2) = −72 + 108 − 36 = 0  ✓

Why that's a breakthrough: a polynomial that vanishes at 1 and 2 must have (z−1)(z−2) as a factor. Call that the vanishing polynomial Z(z). So "all constraints hold" becomes "Z divides P exactly" — and a division either has a remainder or it doesn't, whether there were two constraints or two million.

−18z² + 54z − 36  =  −18(z² − 3z + 2)  =  −18·(z−1)(z−2)

so    Z(z) = (z−1)(z−2)      and      H(z) = −18

Peggy's entire claim has collapsed into one identity:

A(z)·B(z) − C(z)  =  H(z)·Z(z)

Stage 8

Hide the spot Chapter 15, trusted setup

One hole remains. If Peggy learns z before building her polynomials, she can hand-craft curves that happen to agree at that one point and nowhere else — passing the check with a nonsense witness.

So the evaluation point must be a secret nobody knows, including the verifier. The ceremony picks a random τ, publishes gτ, gτ², gτ³…, and destroys τ. From those powers Peggy can compute gA(τ) without ever learning τ — and a pairing lets the gate check the multiplication relation on those hidden values.

That is the toxic waste: anyone who kept τ could forge. Hence the ceremony with thousands of participants, safe if a single one was honest.

What actually ships Chapters 16–17

Same letters, different things

When you read a paper, check whether a letter is a matrix, a polynomial, or a point.

Part V · Chapter 17

Verifier.sol

Where all of Part IV lands: a generated Solidity contract that turns a Groth16 proof into a boolean.

A Groth16 proof is three curve points — A and C on G1, B on G2 — eight field elements, 256 bytes. Verification is one pairing equation:

e(A, B) = e(α, β) · e(vk_x, γ) · e(C, δ)

Where that equation comes from

It looks arbitrary. It isn't — every symbol is there to stop a specific attack. Start with the problem it solves.

In Chapter 15 the prover commits to its polynomials in the exponent: it publishes gA(τ), gB(τ), gC(τ) without ever revealing τ or the witness. But the thing we need to check is A(τ)·B(τ) − C(τ) = H(τ)·Z(τ) — a multiplication of two hidden values. And ordinary group arithmetic cannot do that:

That single property, e(ga, gb) = e(g,g)ab, is called bilinearity, and it is the only thing about pairings you need. It is also why BN254 is used and secp256k1 cannot be: most curves have no efficient pairing.

Building the equation up

Rearrange the QAP so the multiplication is on one side:

A(τ)·B(τ) = C(τ) + H(τ)·Z(τ)

Pair both sides and you get the naive check: e(A, B) = e(C, g). That already works — but it is forgeable. Nothing forces A, B and C to have come from a real witness; a cheat can pick any three values that happen to multiply out.

So Groth16 adds four secret constants from the ceremony. Each one blocks a specific escape:

TermWhat it holdsWhy it is there
e(A, B)the real QAP product — but with α baked into A and β into Bthe actual check from Stage 6 of the Wizard's Gate, now done on hidden values
e(α, β)a constantexpanding (α + A)(β + B) leaves a stray αβ. This cancels it. Precomputed once and hardcoded into your contract — it never changes
e(vk_x, γ)the public inputsthe verifier builds vk_x itself from the public signals. γ keeps that lane separate so the prover can't quietly claim a different public input
e(C, δ)the private witness part, plus H·Zδ stops private terms being mixed into the public half, or vice versa

α and β are what make this an argument of knowledge rather than a mere equation-satisfying puzzle. To produce a valid A and B, the prover must use reference-string elements that entangle α and β with the genuine QAP polynomials. Making up A and B freely would require knowing α and β — and those are the toxic waste, destroyed in the ceremony. This is the precise point at which the trusted setup earns its keep.

What your contract actually runs

The EVM pairing precompile doesn't compare two pairings — it checks whether a product of pairings equals 1. So the generated Solidity moves everything to one side and negates A:

e(−A, B) · e(α, β) · e(vk_x, γ) · e(C, δ) = 1

One batched call to 0x08, four pairings, ~230k gas. And notice the count: four, always. Two constraints or a hundred million, the verifier does identical work. That flatness is not an optimisation — it is the SNARK.

Practically, vk_x is a linear combination the verifier assembles from your public signals — vk_x = IC[0] + Σ input[i]·IC[i], where the IC points come from the verification key. The generated contract is mostly assembly calling three EVM precompiles:

contract Verifier {
    // 0x06 ecAdd · 0x07 ecMul · 0x08 ecPairing  (EIP-196 / EIP-197)
    function verifyProof(
        uint[2] calldata a,
        uint[2][2] calldata b,
        uint[2] calldata c,
        uint[1] calldata input      // your public signals
    ) public view returns (bool);
}

Your application contract calls it and then acts on the result:

function withdraw(Proof calldata p, bytes32 root, bytes32 nullifierHash, address to)
    external
{
    require(isKnownRoot(root),            "unknown merkle root");
    require(!spent[nullifierHash],        "already spent");
    require(verifier.verifyProof(p.a, p.b, p.c,
            [uint(root), uint(nullifierHash), uint(uint160(to))]),
                                         "invalid proof");
    spent[nullifierHash] = true;        // effects before interaction
    payable(to).transfer(DENOMINATION);
}
Four ways to get this wrong
  1. Forgetting a public input. Anything not in input[] is not bound by the proof. Omit to and a MEV bot front-runs your withdrawal, replaying your valid proof to its own address. This is the on-chain face of weak Fiat–Shamir.
  2. No nullifier check. Proofs are replayable by design — they're just bytes. Something stateful must mark them spent.
  3. Unreduced inputs. Public signals must be < r (the BN254 scalar field order). Good generated verifiers check; if yours doesn't, x and x + r are two proofs for the same statement, breaking any uniqueness assumption.
  4. Not pinning the verification key. An upgradeable verifier address is a backdoor that mints money. Immutable, or behind a very slow timelock.

Cost reality check: ~230k gas per Groth16 verification, dominated by the pairing precompile, and it is flat — the same whether the circuit had 1,000 or 100 million constraints. That flatness is the whole economic argument for rollups.

Part V · Chapter 18

Anatomy of a mixer

The canonical ZK application, in full. Two cryptographic ideas — a Merkle tree and a nullifier — solve what looks impossible.

The puzzle: let someone withdraw 1 ETH if and only if they previously deposited 1 ETH, without linking the two transactions, and without letting them withdraw twice. Deposits and withdrawals are both public. How?

Deposit. Pick two random secrets, nullifier k and secret s. Send 1 ETH along with the commitment C = H(k ‖ s). The contract appends C as a leaf in an on-chain Merkle tree. Nothing about you is revealed — C is just a hash.

Withdraw. From a fresh, unfunded address, submit a proof of this statement:

// public:  root, nullifierHash, recipient
// private: k, s, merkle path
C            = H(k, s)
root         == MerkleRoot(C, path)      // my leaf is in the tree
nullifierHash == H(k)                    // derived from the same k
recipient                                // bound in, so the proof can't be stolen

The contract checks the proof, checks nullifierHash is unseen, records it, and pays out. It has no idea which leaf was yours — you proved membership in the whole set. And since nullifierHash is deterministic in k, a second withdrawal from the same deposit collides and is rejected.

Part V · Chapter 19

zkRollups and the zkEVM

The application that put ZK on every roadmap — and notice it uses none of the zero-knowledge, only the succinctness.

Ethereum L1 does ~15 transactions per second because every node re-executes everything. A validity rollup breaks that: execute off-chain, then post a proof that the execution was correct. L1 verifies 200 bytes instead of re-running 5,000 transactions.

Contrast with optimistic rollups: those assume batches are valid and allow a 7-day fraud-proof window. Validity rollups prove it up front — no challenge period, so withdrawals finalise in minutes, not a week, and no honest-watcher assumption.

Naming, honestly

"zkRollup" is a misnomer everyone has given up correcting. Nothing is hidden — every transaction is published for data availability. What's used is succinctness: a small proof for a big computation. The accurate term is validity rollup. Privacy on a rollup (Aztec) is a separate, harder design on top.

zkEVM types

Proving EVM execution is hard because the EVM was designed with no thought for circuits: 256-bit words, Keccak everywhere, a stack machine. Vitalik's taxonomy:

Two Circom rules that cause most beginner bugs

<-- assigns without constraining. <== assigns and constrains. Use <-- only when you must compute something the constraint system can't express directly (like a division or a bit split) — and then you must add the checking constraints yourself with ===. Every unconstrained signal is a hole an attacker fills with whatever value they like.

Signals are immutable and the language is not imperative: the whole template describes a fixed set of equations, evaluated all at once. for loops are unrolled at compile time, and every bound must be a constant.

Part V · Worked example

The Dragon's Hoard

The Wizard's Gate took one statement from English to three numbers. This one takes it the rest of the way — into a deployed contract, through a hostile mempool, and out the other side with the gold.

A dragon dies and leaves a contract holding 10 ETH. Etched into storage is a single number:

commitment = 0x1f3b…c42a
“Whoever knows the secret behind this hash may take the hoard.”

You know the secret. So does nobody else. This should be easy.

Stage 1

The obvious way, and why you lose everything

The naive contract takes the secret directly:

function claim(uint256 secret) external {
    require(poseidon(secret) == commitment, "wrong secret");
    payable(msg.sender).transfer(address(this).balance);
}

You send the transaction. It lands in the public mempool, where your calldata — including secret — is readable by every searcher on the network. A bot copies it, resubmits with a higher priority fee, and its transaction mines first.

The dragon's gold is gone, and you paid gas to tell a robot the password. This is not a hypothetical; it is the default outcome for any puzzle contract on a public chain.

The trap is structural: revealing the secret and using the secret are the same action. Zero knowledge exists to split those two apart.

Stage 2

Write the statement down first

Before any code, say precisely what must be proved — the shape from Chapter 1:

// public — everyone sees these, they go on chain
commitment      the number etched in storage
nullifierHash   a one-way fingerprint of this claim, so it can't be reused
recipient       where the gold should go

// private — the witness, never leaves your laptop
secret          the preimage
nullifier       a second random value you chose

Two of those deserve a note, because they are the whole security design:

  • nullifierHash — the contract records it after a successful claim. A second claim with the same one reverts. Proofs are just bytes and are infinitely replayable; something stateful has to burn them.
  • recipient — bound into the proof itself. This is what makes the proof useless to anyone but you, and it is the fix for Stage 1.
Stage 3

The circuit

Three jobs, about forty lines including imports:

// claim.circom
pragma circom 2.1.6;
include "circomlib/circuits/poseidon.circom";

template ClaimHoard() {
    signal input secret;          // private
    signal input nullifier;       // private
    signal input commitment;      // public
    signal input nullifierHash;   // public
    signal input recipient;       // public

    // 1 · I know the preimage of the commitment
    component h = Poseidon(2);
    h.inputs[0] <== secret;
    h.inputs[1] <== nullifier;
    commitment === h.out;

    // 2 · the nullifier hash really comes from my nullifier
    component n = Poseidon(1);
    n.inputs[0] <== nullifier;
    nullifierHash === n.out;

    // 3 · bind the recipient into the proof
    signal recipientSq;
    recipientSq <== recipient * recipient;
}

component main {public [commitment, nullifierHash, recipient]} = ClaimHoard();
That third block is not decoration

recipient is never used in any real calculation — so the compiler would happily optimise it away, and a signal that appears in no constraint is not actually bound to the proof. The dummy recipient * recipient forces it into the constraint system. Tornado Cash carries exactly this line for exactly this reason. Delete it and you are back to Stage 1, but with a false sense of security.

Part V · Chapter 21

The bug that eats ZK projects

Almost every serious ZK vulnerability found in production has the same root cause. It is not in the cryptography.

The maths in Parts III and IV is solid. Groth16 has machine-checked proofs. What breaks is the circuit: the gap between the computation you meant to constrain and the one you actually constrained. Two shapes:

Under-constrained circuits

A signal that isn't fully pinned down. The classic is a boolean that was never forced to be boolean:

// BROKEN — b can be anything, e.g. 7, or p−1
signal input b;
out <== b * x + (1 - b) * y;

// FIXED — this single line forces b ∈ {0,1}
b * (b - 1) === 0;

With b = 7 the selector outputs 7x − 6y: a perfectly valid proof of a completely different statement. The proof verifies; your invariant is gone. Related classics: forgetting range checks so a value wraps mod p (a "1 ETH" withdrawal of p+1), and non-canonical field element encodings.

Over-constrained circuits

The reverse — an accidental constraint that makes some honest inputs unprovable. Not a theft risk, but a liveness bug: users' funds get stuck with no way to generate a valid proof. Found by fuzzing across the whole input domain, not by testing the happy path.

A review checklist you can actually use
  1. Is every signal written with <-- accompanied by constraints that uniquely determine it?
  2. Is every boolean constrained with b*(b−1)===0?
  3. Is every value that must fit in n bits range-checked? (Num2Bits — and is n small enough to prevent aliasing?)
  4. Do the public inputs include everything that must be bound — recipient, nonce, chain id, contract address?
  5. Is there a nullifier, and is it marked spent before any external call?
  6. Does the proof bind the verification key / circuit version, so proofs can't be replayed across circuits or chains?
  7. Was the ceremony transcript published, and can you verify your own contribution?
  8. Have you run circomspect and, for anything holding real money, a formal tool (Ecne, Picus) plus an audit by a ZK-specialist firm?

Capstone · a real system

Proving you're 18 without saying your age

Everything in this course, assembled into one working project: a private student registry, a circom circuit, a Solidity gate, and an attack suite that shows precisely where the cryptography stops and your judgement starts.

The brief. A college holds a registry of 10 students — name, roll number, class, age. A course is restricted to 18+. The college must prove a student qualifies without revealing their age or any other detail, and the course provider must be able to verify it without inspecting the registry.

Decision 1 · who actually holds the prover role

The obvious reading is that the college proves. Resist it. If the college generates proofs on demand it learns which student applies where, and the provider has to trust it live, forever. Split the roles instead:

The sentence this project exists to teach

The circuit enforces consistency with a commitment. Choosing which commitment and which threshold to trust is the verifier's job, and no amount of cryptography does it for you. A gas trap worth knowing

A malformed proof point — one not on the curve — reverts, but only after burning the entire gas budget. The snarkjs verifier runs checkField on the public signals, but proof points go straight to ecPairing, which on invalid input fails and consumes all gas forwarded to it. Not exploitable, since the attacker pays — but a user with a buggy proving client loses a whole transaction instead of getting a cheap revert. Verify proofs off-chain before submitting.

What it does not protect

Worth stating plainly, because a system like this is easy to oversell:

  • ZK does not make the college honest. It proves a claim is consistent with what the college committed to — never that the commitment is true. A corrupt registrar can write age 99 for a 15-year-old, and the proof will be flawless. Cryptography secures the channel, not the source. Real deployments anchor to a government credential instead.
  • Plain ages go stale. age: 17 is wrong on the next birthday and the whole tree must be rebuilt. Store a date of birth and prove dob ≤ cutoff, with the contract deriving the cutoff from block.timestamp.
  • Roll numbers are public here by design. That matches a provider who needs to check "this particular student". Drop the roll number and add a nullifier for anonymity — but with 10 students the anonymity set is nearly worthless, which is Chapter 18's metadata lesson again.
  • A self-run ceremony is not a ceremony. Generating your own powers of tau means you know the toxic waste. Reuse the Perpetual Powers of Tau transcript.

That list is not a disclaimer. Knowing exactly which of your guarantees come from mathematics and which come from a trust assumption is the skill this course was teaching.

Labs use deliberately tiny parameters (p = 1019, 𝔽₉₇, 4-bit hashes) so you can follow every number by hand. Real systems use 254-bit primes. The structure is identical; only the size of the dial changes.
